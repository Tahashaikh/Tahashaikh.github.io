Best AI Tools for Restaurants in 2026: Phones & Bookings

Where AI genuinely helps a restaurant with thin margins — missed-call recovery, reservations, review responses, and demand forecasting — and what to ignore.

By Muhammad Taha Tariq , Software Automation Engineer & Editor Published October 23, 2026 Updated October 24, 2026 4 min read

Restaurant margins are thin enough that AI has to pay for itself in weeks, not quarters. That rules out most of the category immediately. What survives is the small set of tools that either recover lost revenue or remove a repetitive task from an already-stretched team.

This guide is ordered by measurable return for an independent restaurant, not by how impressive the demo is.

1. Phone answering: the clearest win

A restaurant misses calls during service. Every missed call is a lost booking, and nobody ever sees the loss because it never appears in the books.

AI phone assistants built for restaurants — Slang.ai is the best-known — answer immediately, handle hours, location, menu questions, and reservations, and hand off to a human for anything unusual. The worthwhile ones integrate with your reservation book so a booking made by voice actually appears in the system.

What to demand before you buy:

  • Reservation system integration. If it cannot write to your book, it is a glorified answering machine.
  • A human fallback. Large parties, allergies, complaints, and private events go to a person.
  • Call recordings and transcripts. You need to hear how it handles real calls in week one.
  • A simple way to turn it off. During a soft opening or a busy holiday, you may want a human.

The measurement is simple: phone bookings before and after, plus the number of calls answered outside service hours. Most restaurants see the case in the first month.

2. Reservations and no-shows

OpenTable and Resy already use data to predict no-shows and manage waitlists, and their AI features are improvements to a system you likely already use. The practical gains:

  • Better no-show prediction, so you can overbook slightly with confidence.
  • Guest history that the host can see at the door.
  • Automated confirmations and waitlist fills.

The AI is not the reason to choose a platform — coverage and commission structure are — but if you are choosing, weigh how well the prediction actually matches your covers.

3. Food cost and inventory

This is the category with the biggest theoretical upside and the biggest data problem.

Tools like MarketMan turn purchasing and sales data into food-cost reporting, and modern POS systems such as Toast add forecasting. The catch: these tools are only as good as the invoices you enter. If deliveries are keyed in inconsistently, forecasts will be confidently wrong.

Sequence that works:

  1. Standardise how invoices are entered. One method, everyone.
  2. Run a full stock count for four weeks to establish a baseline.
  3. Then turn on forecasting and compare predicted vs actual usage.
  4. Chase the largest variance first — usually a handful of proteins, not the whole menu.

4. Labour and scheduling

7shifts and similar platforms use sales history to suggest staffing levels and handle schedule communication. In a labour-tight market, the scheduling and shift-swap features often matter more than the forecasting. The AI suggestion is a starting point; a manager who knows the team still makes the schedule.

5. Reviews and marketing

Review responses are a genuine AI use case because they are high-volume and emotionally draining. Ask a model to draft a response that:

  • Names the specific issue the reviewer raised.
  • Says what you are doing about it.
  • Avoids defensiveness and avoids invented facts.
  • Stays short.

Then edit it. The generic “We’re sorry you feel that way” reply is worse than no reply, and AI produces it by default unless you push back.

For social posts, AI is fine for captions and scheduling copy. For food photography, use real photos of your real dishes. AI-generated food images are increasingly recognisable and they misrepresent the product in a way diners punish.

What to skip

  • AI “dynamic pricing” experiments that change menu prices by demand. Guests notice, and the backlash is rarely worth the margin.
  • Fully automated review management with no edit step. Insincere replies are worse than silence.
  • AI menu design tools if you have a designer or a template. The output looks generic.
  • Chatbots on your website before your phone is answered. Fix the phone first.

The bottom line

Restaurant AI works when it is pointed at revenue you can count: answered calls, filled tables, controlled food cost. It fails when it is pointed at the guest experience — dynamic prices, generated food photos, canned review replies. Fix the phone, clean the purchasing data, and let the rest wait until the first two are paying for themselves.

Frequently asked questions

What is the highest-ROI AI tool for a restaurant?

For most independent restaurants it is AI phone answering, because a missed call during service is a lost booking. A voice assistant that answers hours, takes reservations, and answers menu questions recovers revenue that is otherwise silently lost. It is also the easiest to measure: compare bookings from phone before and after.

Can AI take reservations reliably?

Yes, for standard bookings, if it is connected to your actual reservation system and has a clear fallback to a human for large parties, special requests, and anything unusual. The failure mode is a bot that confirms a table it cannot actually hold. Test the edge cases before going live.

Is AI useful for menu costing and inventory?

It is useful for forecasting and for turning your sales and purchase data into a weekly prep plan. It does not fix bad data. If invoices are not entered consistently, forecasting tools will produce confident nonsense. Clean up purchasing data first.

Should restaurants use AI to reply to reviews?

Yes, as a drafting aid. AI writes a calm, specific response faster than most owners can at midnight, but the reply should still be edited. Generic AI responses ("We are sorry for your experience") read as insincere and can make a bad review worse.
