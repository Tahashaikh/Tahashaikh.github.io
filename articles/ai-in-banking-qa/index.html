AI in Banking QA: What Works, What Compliance Blocks

Where AI actually helps a bank testing team, where DORA and model-risk expectations stop it, and the data and evidence rules to settle before you buy a tool.

By Muhammad Taha Tariq , Software Automation Engineer & Editor Published October 24, 2026 Updated October 24, 2026 5 min read

AI can draft tests and sort failures. It cannot sign off a release, replace audit evidence, or validate a model.

Banking QA teams are not slow because testers are slow. They are slow because every release needs traceability (a clear link from requirement to test to result), approved test data, independent evidence, and a final approval that survives an audit. AI can compress the drafting and triage work. It cannot compress the accountability.

This is the practical split: what AI can do inside a regulated bank, what it cannot, and the questions to settle before a vendor demo.

This is an editorial guide, not regulatory advice. DORA applies to EU financial entities; other jurisdictions have their own rules. Confirm obligations with your compliance and risk functions.

What regulators actually require

DORA (EU 2022/2554) has applied since 17 January 2025. Article 25 requires a testing programme. It includes, as appropriate: vulnerability scans, open-source analyses, network security assessments, gap analyses, physical security reviews, source code reviews where feasible, scenario-based tests, compatibility testing, performance testing, end-to-end testing, and penetration testing.

The Austrian regulator’s guidance adds the practical expectations. Test systems that support critical functions at least once a year. Classify and fix weaknesses. Keep the evidence ready for supervisors.

DORA RTS 2024/1774, Article 16 is the clause most test teams feel first. Non-production environments should hold only anonymised, pseudonymised, or randomised production data. Production data in a test environment is an exception: specific occasions, limited time, approval by the relevant function, and reported to the ICT risk function.

Model risk guidance (SR 11-7, and the revised SR 26-2 issued 17 April 2026) sets the validation shape: conceptual soundness, ongoing monitoring, outcomes analysis, and independent challenge. If an AI system is used in a decision that affects customers or risk, expect the same treatment: documentation, testing, monitoring, and a named owner.

The pattern that works is AI drafts, a human decides, the system records. The pattern that fails is an AI output becoming the evidence.

Where it does not fit

  • Final release approval. A named person still owns it. It cannot be given to a model.
  • Regulatory evidence. Auditors need versioned artifacts, not a chat transcript.
  • Production customer data. Do not paste it into a general assistant. This is the fastest way to create a reportable incident.
  • Model validation. If the AI influences a decision, validation and monitoring are a separate discipline.
  • Risk acceptance. The tool can rank risks; the risk owner accepts them.

The data rule, in practice

Before any pilot, settle five things in writing:

  1. Where the data lives. Region, tenancy, subprocessors, retention.
  2. Whether your data trains the vendor’s models. Get a contractual no, not a marketing page.
  3. What is logged. Prompt and output logging is required for your evidence trail, and also a privacy exposure. Decide who can read it.
  4. How deletion works. Test data, prompts, screenshots, and any fine-tuned artifacts.
  5. Which environment is allowed. DORA’s default is anonymised, pseudonymised, or randomised data in non-production.

If a vendor cannot answer these in a security questionnaire without a sales call, that is the answer.

A workflow that survives audit

  1. Requirement to test. AI drafts cases from the approved requirement. A human reviews and links each case to the requirement ID.
  2. Data. Cases run only on approved non-production data. The data source and masking method are recorded.
  3. Execution. CI runs the suite; results attach traces, logs, and environment metadata.
  4. Triage. AI summarises the failure; the engineer confirms the root cause.
  5. Evidence. The traceability matrix, test results, and final approval are exported and versioned.
  6. Change control. When the AI tool or model version changes, record it and re-run a control set.

That last step is easy to skip and hard to defend later.

Questions to ask a vendor

  • Do you have a validation or security pack my risk team can review?
  • Can we export tests, results, and evidence in a machine-readable format?
  • Can we pin the model version and see when it changes?
  • Do you log prompts and outputs, and can we control retention?
  • Which subprocessors touch our data, and in which regions?
  • What happens to our data if we leave?

Who this is not for

If your test data is still copied from production without controls, fix that first. AI on top of an uncontrolled data path increases the blast radius; it does not improve quality.

The bottom line

AI is useful in banking QA where the output is a draft that a named human reviews: cases, coverage analysis, triage summaries, and choosing which old tests to run. It is not useful as a substitute for evidence, final approval, or model validation (checking that a model works as intended). Approve the data path and the evidence trail before you approve the licence.

Frequently asked questions

Can banks use AI tools to generate test cases?

Yes, with controls. The common pattern is AI-assisted drafting of cases and coverage analysis, followed by human review and traceability to requirements. The tool must not receive production customer data, and the evidence must remain exportable and versioned for auditors.

Does DORA require AI testing tools?

No. DORA requires a digital operational resilience testing programme, including vulnerability assessments, source code reviews where feasible, scenario-based tests, performance testing, end-to-end testing and penetration testing. It does not mandate AI. AI is one way to produce or maintain those tests; the obligations are about coverage, independence and evidence.

Is a test-generation tool a model under SR 11-7?

Usually not, because it does not produce a quantitative output used for a business decision. But if the AI output influences a credit, risk, or customer decision, or your model inventory policy defines it as a model, validation expectations can apply. That classification is for your risk function to make, not the vendor.

What is the biggest blocker to AI adoption in banking QA?

Data. Test environments are supposed to hold anonymised, pseudonymised or randomised production data, and most AI tools are cloud services. Until the data path, retention, training use and audit logging are approved, the tool cannot be used on realistic scenarios.
