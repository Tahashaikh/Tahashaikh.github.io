Best AI Tools for Dentists in 2026: Radiographs & Comms

A practical guide to AI in dentistry: which radiograph AI is FDA-cleared, what practice-management AI does, and where patient comms tools earn their fee.

By Muhammad Taha Tariq , Software Automation Engineer & Editor Published October 19, 2026 Updated October 24, 2026 5 min read

Dental practices are sold a lot of AI right now, and most of it is not for them. The market splits into two very different products: radiographic AI, which is clinically regulated and genuinely useful but narrow, and operational AI, which is unglamorous, cheap, and where most of the return actually comes from.

This guide separates the two, tells you which radiographic tools carry FDA clearance, and explains where a small practice should spend first.

Clinical note: nothing here is clinical advice. Radiographic AI tools are cleared as aids to a licensed dentist, not replacements. Always confirm clearance status and indications on the FDA’s own database and the vendor’s documentation before you buy.

Radiographic AI: what it is cleared to do

Three names come up in almost every evaluation: Overjet, Pearl, and VideaHealth. All three have FDA-cleared claims for detecting findings such as interproximal caries on bitewing and periapical images. Additional features vary by product and clearance, so check the current FDA listing for the exact indications before you rely on any of them.

What they do well:

  • Consistency. A tired associate at 6pm reads a bitewing differently than the same associate at 9am. The model does not get tired.
  • Patient communication. Highlighted findings on a screen are far more persuasive than a dentist saying “there is something here”. Overjet in particular leans into patient-facing visuals, which shortens the “why do I need this filling” conversation.
  • Measurement. Where the product includes measurement overlays, they make it easier to track bone level or lesion size over time. Confirm the feature is inside the cleared indication before relying on it.

What they do not do:

  • Replace the clinical exam, or bill on your behalf, or decide a treatment plan.
  • Work perfectly on poor-quality sensors or badly positioned films. Garbage in, garbage out still applies.

If you are a single-location practice, the honest question is not “is this accurate?” — the evidence is reasonable — but “will my team change behaviour because of it?” A tool that highlights findings the dentist already sees adds cost without adding value. The practices that benefit are the ones that use the overlay during the exam, in front of the patient.

Operational AI: where the money actually is

A dental office runs on phone calls. Recall, reminders, reactivation of lapsed patients, insurance questions, rescheduling. Front-desk staff burn hours on this, and every missed call is a missed chair.

Patient communication platforms — Weave, Lighthouse360, RevenueWell — have added AI-assisted messaging, smart reminders, and increasingly AI phone answering. The measurable effects are:

  • Fewer no-shows when reminders are timed and confirmed rather than blasted once.
  • More reactivated patients when lapsed lists are worked automatically instead of manually.
  • Less phone time per booking.

This is the least exciting AI purchase in dentistry and usually the one with the clearest ROI. If a practice can only buy one thing this year, buy this, not radiograph AI.

The rule for AI phone agents

If you deploy an AI voice agent to answer the phone, set hard boundaries:

  1. It books, reschedules, and answers hours and location questions. It does not give clinical advice.
  2. It hands off to a human for anything involving pain, emergencies, insurance disputes, or a complaint.
  3. Every call is recorded and reviewable, and patients are told.

A dental office that lets an AI improvise with a patient in pain will create a problem that costs more than the subscription saves.

Charting and dictation

Clinical notes are the other recurring time sink. Dragon Medical One remains the mature option for dictation into most practice-management systems, and the newer general assistants can draft patient-friendly explanations of a procedure if you feed them the facts.

The pattern that works:

  • Dictate or type the clinical facts yourself.
  • Let a model turn those facts into a patient-friendly paragraph for a treatment plan or follow-up email.
  • Read it before it goes out.

Do not let a model invent clinical detail. If the note says “MOD composite, 14”, the model should not be adding “patient tolerated well” unless you told it that.

Practice management systems

Dentrix, Eaglesoft, and Open Dental are all adding AI-flavoured features: smarter scheduling suggestions, claim scrubbing, and reporting assistants. The right move is almost always to exhaust what your existing system already includes before buying a standalone tool. Ask your rep two questions:

  • What AI features are included in our current plan, and are we using them?
  • Which features cost extra, and what does the upgrade actually change in the schedule?

Most practices discover they are paying for modules nobody turned on.

What to skip

  • Standalone AI marketing copy generators. A general assistant does this for a fraction of the cost.
  • AI “diagnosis” apps that are not FDA-cleared. If it is making clinical claims without clearance, walk away.
  • Enterprise radiographic AI for a single chair. The clinical value is real but the volume is too low to justify the contract.
  • Any tool that will not sign a BAA. This is non-negotiable under HIPAA.

For a single-location practice, start with communication and dictation. Add radiographic AI when you have the clinical workflow to use it chairside, and when the case-presentation benefit is something your team will actually lean on.

The bottom line

Dental AI is not one decision. The clinically regulated half is a consistency tool for practices that will use it in front of patients. The operational half is a phone-and-recall tool that pays for itself. Buy in that order, insist on a BAA, and keep every clinical judgement with a licensed dentist.

Frequently asked questions

Is dental AI allowed to diagnose cavities?

No. The FDA-cleared radiographic tools are cleared as aids to the dentist, not as autonomous diagnosticians. They highlight and quantify findings such as interproximal caries or bone loss, and the licensed dentist remains responsible for the diagnosis and treatment plan. Treat the output as a second set of eyes, not a verdict.

What is the biggest AI time-saver in a dental office?

For most practices it is not radiograph analysis — it is patient communication. Automated recall, reminder, and reactivation messaging reduces front-desk phone time and no-shows, and the effect shows up within the first month. Radiographic AI improves consistency but does not remove a task from the schedule.

Do small practices need an enterprise dental AI platform?

Usually not. A single-chair or two-chair practice gets most of the benefit from the AI features already bundled into its practice-management system plus a patient communication platform. Enterprise radiographic AI makes more sense for multi-location groups where consistency across clinicians is the goal.

How should a practice handle patient data with AI tools?

Only use vendors that will sign a business associate agreement, and keep patient data out of consumer chatbots entirely. Verify where data is stored, whether it is used for model training, and how it is deleted. Your HIPAA obligations do not change because a tool is new.
